## Saudi Arabia's Dark-Fleet Shuttle Is Quietly Deflating the Hormuz Risk Premium

<cite index="38-1">Oil fell for a third session and finished the week flat, as the market decided the pipeline closure won't hit supply as hard as first feared</cite>. That's because <cite index="19-3">tankers now load inside the Gulf, run through Hormuz under US naval escort, then transfer cargo to long-haul VLCCs off Oman's Sohar port</cite>. <cite index="20-1,20-2">Saudi has sold roughly 60 million barrels this way for September-October delivery, pushing exports back near pre-crisis levels and cooling prices</cite>. The catch: it's a congested, fragile relay — one attack away from erasing the calm.

## Update: A War-Crimes Finding Just Made Trump's UN Gulf Meeting Harder to Spin as an Off-Ramp

<cite index="28-1,28-3">A UN fact-finding mission concluded there are reasonable grounds to believe US forces committed war crimes in February strikes on a school and sports complex, and the White House rejected the report</cite>. The timing matters: this lands right before <cite index="29-10">a reported Gulf Arab-US-Iran meeting around the UN General Assembly next week</cite>. Any concession there now reads domestically as bowing to a body Washington just called biased — raising the odds Tuesday produces posturing over de-escalation, the opposite of what's priced into the Hormuz risk premium.

## Forget the Next Outage — Gulf Oil's Tom Kloza Says $3 Diesel Is Gone for 15 Months

<cite index="44-1">Kloza argues prices can keep rising over the next 100 days because the world has lost roughly 7 million bpd of refining capacity — mostly Russian plants hit by Ukrainian drones and Persian Gulf refineries caught in the war — and that $3 diesel is gone for at least 15 to 16 months</cite>. That reframes every headline outage — Joliet, Aramco's pipeline, Russian strikes — as symptoms of a structural deficit, not isolated events. Anyone budgeting for a quick post-winter normalization in diesel and jet fuel is almost certainly wrong.
