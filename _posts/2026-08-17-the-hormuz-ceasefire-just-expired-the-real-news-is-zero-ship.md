## The Hormuz Ceasefire Just Expired. The Real News Is Zero Ships Crossed Sunday.

The 60-day US-Iran ceasefire lapsed today, but the sharper signal is that <cite index="20-7,20-8">only five cargo ships passed through the strait on Saturday and none were registered to pass on Sunday, compared with 31 vessels the previous weekend</cite>. That means the closure is already priced into markets—a formal ceasefire collapse changes little in physical flows. The bigger swing factor is that <cite index="19-12">Tehran is working on a separate agreement with Oman to restore traffic through the Strait of Hormuz</cite>, a deal Washington isn't party to. If that lands, barrels could move again without Trump securing anything he's demanded.

## Trump Wants to Own the Strait of Hormuz. Iran and Oman May Just Reopen It Without Him.

<cite index="37-2,37-3,37-4">Trump has announced plans to declare the Strait of Hormuz a 'territory' of the US, telling supporters the US would soon claim the waterway critical for global oil supply</cite>. Legally it goes nowhere: <cite index="39-1">Oman and Iran share jurisdiction of the waterway</cite>, not Washington. More consequential: <cite index="19-12">Tehran is working on a separate agreement with Oman to restore traffic through the strait</cite>. If Muscat and Tehran strike that deal first, Hormuz could reopen entirely outside US leverage—making the territorial theater irrelevant to whether crude actually starts flowing.

## A Single Tanker's War-Risk Insurance Now Costs More Than $20 Million a Voyage

<cite index="45-9">A 270,000-DWT crude tanker valued at $210 million can pay roughly $21 million in additional war-risk premium for a single high-tension Hormuz transit</cite>—call it near $10 per barrel of cargo before the crude even reaches a refinery. This isn't a spike that snaps back when headlines calm: <cite index="45-14">insurers are shifting to voyage-by-voyage underwriting due to volatility</cite>. Refiners modeling a 'return to normal' Hormuz should price in a permanently higher landed-cost floor, not pre-war freight economics.
