## DOJ Wants to Turn Seized Iranian Tankers Into Treasury Revenue — Using a Law Dormant Since the 1800s

<cite index="24-1">The Justice Department is seeking to revive a maritime court system largely unused since the Spanish-American War to streamline seizing Iranian oil tankers</cite>. <cite index="20-4">The Houston-based U.S. Attorney coordinating with the Pentagon confirmed the revival of prize law</cite>, which <cite index="19-7">would let the government sell oil or cargo from ships that violated the blockade, with proceeds going to the Treasury</cite>. <cite index="20-7">Because Congress never declared war on Iran, the legal footing is untested, creating grey area that could pull lawmakers into a conflict they've largely avoided</cite> — raising the stakes for anyone still moving Iran-linked crude.

## US Oil Rigs Just Fell Again — Even as Diamondback Crossed 1 Million Barrels a Day and Doubled Its Buyback

<cite index="100-12">Oil rigs decreased by five to 447 this week</cite>, even as <cite index="112-1">Diamondback's total production exceeded 1 million boe per day for the first time in company history</cite>, and it <cite index="109-1">raised oil-production guidance while keeping full-year cash capital expenditures unchanged at roughly $3.9 billion</cite>. <cite index="109-2">In July, the board doubled the share repurchase authorization to $16.0 billion from $8.0 billion</cite>. Fewer rigs, higher output, cash to shareholders instead of the drill bit — that's discipline beating OPEC+'s assumption that shale will fully answer higher prices.

## Kuwait's Ports Lost 86% of Their Traffic in the War — Singapore and Malaysia Are the Ones Who Gained It

<cite index="48-8">Crude exports from the Gulf region have dropped by nearly half compared with before the war, from about 17 million barrels a day in 2025 to roughly nine million bpd as of August 2026</cite>. But <cite index="48-13">Kuwait saw the steepest fall, with daily port calls dropping by 86 percent</cite>, while <cite index="48-11">Russia's fuel oil shipments to Singapore and Malaysia rose 2.5 times month-on-month in July</cite>. That's not a detour — it's new storage and transshipment infrastructure that won't vanish even if Hormuz fully reopens, permanently shifting trade toward Southeast Asia.
