## CENTCOM Just Built a Permanent Drone War Command. That's Not a Sign the Oil Crisis Ends Soon.

Markets keep pricing Hormuz as a deal away from resolution. CENTCOM's actions say otherwise. The command just stood up Task Force Falcon Strike, a multinational drone unit built to operate for years, not weeks. Unlike the ad hoc Scorpion Strike squadron it replaces, Falcon Strike is explicitly designed to scale with regional partners over time. That's an infrastructure decision, not a bridge measure. If the Pentagon is institutionalizing drone warfare across the Gulf, traders betting on a near-term Hormuz normalization are reading the wrong signal entirely.

## Russia's Spare Oil Capacity Isn't Shut In Anymore — It's Being Permanently Retired

The bullish case for a 2027 Russian supply rebound just got weaker. Rystad now pegs Russia's spare capacity at only 620,000 bpd in 2026, and warns much of it sits on aging, high-water-cut wells shut in under sanctions pressure. The longer those wells stay idle, the more likely they never restart — Rystad flags rising odds of permanent abandonment as repair costs outrun the economics. That reframes every 'Russia rebounds once sanctions ease' forecast: the barrels assumed to be waiting in reserve may simply not exist anymore.

## The Jones Act Waiver Just Turned 240 Days Old — and California Is the Only Real Winner

What began as an emergency 60-day measure has quietly become the longest cabotage exemption in Jones Act history, now requiring voyage-by-voyage Pentagon sign-off. The declared beneficiary was national fuel security; the actual beneficiary is California, where Gulf-to-West-Coast cargoes save 6.6 cents a gallon versus the 2-cent national average. Gulf Coast refiners now face days-long approval delays for a policy mainly propping up one region's supply gap — a preview of how 'temporary' wartime exemptions become structural favoritism the longer a crisis drags on.
