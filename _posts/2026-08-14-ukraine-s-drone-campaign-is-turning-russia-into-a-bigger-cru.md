## Ukraine's Drone Campaign Is Turning Russia Into a Bigger Crude Exporter, Not a Smaller One

Ukraine hit a major Russian refinery deep inside the country this week — <cite index="20-1">the fourth attack in three days</cite> in Kyiv's campaign against Moscow's oil sector. The consequence is counterintuitive: <cite index="59-11">Ukrainian attacks have disrupted at least 17% of Russia's oil refining capacity</cite>, yet rather than starving global markets, <cite index="59-10">Russia has revised its August oil export plan from western ports upward after the drone attacks disabled refineries and freed up more crude for export</cite>. Expect wider Urals discounts and more raw barrels chasing Asian buyers, even as Russian motorists face fuel lines at home.

## OPEC and IEA Just Cut Demand Forecasts for the Fourth Straight Month — the Supply-Crisis Story Has a Hole in It

While every headline has centered on barrels stuck behind Hormuz, OPEC and the IEA just flagged a quieter problem: demand is buckling under the price the war created. <cite index="75-6">OPEC lowered its 2026 global oil demand growth forecast to 580,000 barrels per day, marking its fourth consecutive downward revision</cite>, while <cite index="74-12">the IEA said it expected a contraction of 1.6 million bpd in consumption this year, versus a forecast of 1 million bpd last month</cite>. If elevated prices are destroying demand faster than the conflict is destroying supply, the next market surprise could be a downside one.

## Chevron's Venezuela Bet Just Crossed 280,000 bpd — But It's Built on a Signature, Not a Statute

Chevron has quietly become the biggest wager in the Venezuela reopening story. <cite index="46-1">Chevron has raised Venezuelan output to about 280,000 bpd under a US license as BP, Eni, Shell and Repsol follow and Trump credits his administration.</cite> With Hormuz still bottling up Gulf barrels, majors are treating Venezuelan heavy crude as swing supply. But <cite index="46-5">the risk is that a licensing regime built on executive discretion offers little long-term certainty, a tension several companies have flagged publicly.</cite> One policy reversal could erase this supply cushion overnight.
